# Thought Leadership Effectiveness Reference

Thought leadership is distinct from articles, blog posts, or op-eds. It must establish the author as someone worth listening to on this topic, and reward the reader for their time with insight they can act on.

## Six Criteria

### 1. Clear Position

The author takes a stand. Not "there are pros and cons" but "here's what I believe and why."

**Flag when:**
- The piece ends without a clear thesis
- The author hedges every conclusion into meaninglessness
- The "position" is just a description of the landscape
- Two contradictory positions are presented without the author choosing

### 2. Evidence

Claims backed by data, experience, named examples, or concrete cases.

**Flag when:**
- Assertions float without support
- "Data" is referenced but not shown
- Examples are hypothetical ("imagine a company that...")
- The author's own experience is relevant but not cited
- Statistics are presented without source, date, or methodology

### 3. Counterintuitive Element

At least one surprise that earns the reader's attention. Something that challenges assumptions.

**Flag when:**
- Every point confirms what the reader already believes
- The piece could be generated by autocomplete (predictable next-sentence)
- No "wait, really?" moment anywhere in the piece
- The counterintuitive claim is there but buried or under-emphasized

### 4. Audience Value

Would someone share this? Does it change how they think or act?

**Flag when:**
- The piece is self-promotional disguised as insight
- Takeaways are too abstract to act on ("leaders should be more innovative")
- The audience isn't defined — who is this FOR?
- Value is asserted rather than delivered ("this framework will transform your business" without showing how)

### 5. Authority

Demonstrated expertise, not just opinions. Earned through specifics.

**Flag when:**
- Claims of expertise without evidence of it
- Generic advice anyone could give
- Missing: specific numbers, named projects, concrete outcomes, lessons from failure
- The author hasn't earned the right to make the claims they're making

### 6. So What?

The piece lands on actionable implications, not just observation.

**Flag when:**
- Ends with a vague call to action ("organizations should consider...")
- Describes a problem without proposing solutions or next steps
- The conclusion doesn't connect back to why the reader started reading
- Implications are stated at too high a level to be useful

## Thought Leadership Anti-Patterns

| Pattern | What It Looks Like | The Problem |
|---------|-------------------|-------------|
| "Insight theatre" | Impressive-sounding but empty frameworks | No actionable takeaway. Reader feels cheated. |
| "Trend surfing" | "AI/blockchain/ESG will change everything" | Says nothing specific. Could be anyone's article. |
| "Humble brag" | "When I was advising the Fortune 500 CEO..." | Credibility through name-dropping, not substance. |
| "Survey says" | "78% of leaders agree that X is important" | Survey of opinions isn't evidence of truth. |
| "The listicle" | "7 Ways to Transform Your Business" | Breadth without depth. No single point lands. |
| "The history lesson" | Three pages of background before any insight | Reader loses patience before the payoff. |
| "Both sides" | "There are valid perspectives on each side" | Fence-sitting isn't leadership. Pick a side. |
