# Argument Quality Reference

Based on Paul Graham's "How to Write Usefully" — the four components multiply together for overall usefulness. A zero in any dimension makes the whole piece useless.

## The Four Components

### 1. Correctness

"Useful writing makes claims that are as strong as they can be made without becoming false."

**Review for:**
- Claims stated as fact without evidence or qualification
- Vague statements that are "correct" but tell the reader nothing ("this is a complex issue with many factors")
- Overgeneralizations that collapse under scrutiny
- Precise-sounding numbers used without sources
- Causal claims where only correlation exists

**The precision/correctness tradeoff:** It's easy to be correct by being vague. It's easy to be precise by being wrong. Good writing is both precise AND correct. Flag passages that sacrifice one for the other.

### 2. Novelty

"Useful writing tells people something true and important that they didn't already know."

**Review for:**
- Restating conventional wisdom without adding to it
- Ideas the target audience has heard many times before
- "Insights" that are actually definitions or descriptions
- Missing the unconscious-knowledge opportunity — things readers know but haven't articulated
- Failure to identify what's genuinely new in the author's perspective

**The novelty test:** After reading, can you state something you now know that you didn't before? If not, the piece lacks novelty.

### 3. Importance

Importance = (number of people affected) × (how much it matters to them).

**Review for:**
- Niche topics presented as universal without bridging to broader relevance
- Missing the "so what?" — why should readers care?
- Buried lede — the most important point hiding in paragraph 7
- Importance assumed rather than demonstrated
- Scale mismatch — treating a minor issue as critical, or a critical issue as routine

### 4. Strength

Ideas stated as boldly as possible while remaining accurate, using skillful qualification rather than hedging.

**Review for:**
- Over-qualification: "Perhaps it might be the case that sometimes..." — just say it
- Under-qualification: Bold claims stated without the necessary caveats
- Weak verbs: "seems to", "appears to", "tends to" — when the author actually knows
- Mealy-mouthed conclusions that don't commit to a position
- Misuse of "I think" — appropriate for genuine uncertainty, not for avoiding commitment

**Graham on qualification:** "Qualifications are not scalars... there must be 50 things they can express." Good qualification is a skill, not a tax. Flag both over-qualification (cowardice) and under-qualification (recklessness).

**Understate rather than overstate (Roman).** It is more persuasive to understate than to overstate. A single obvious exaggeration in an otherwise careful argument arouses suspicion of the entire case. Flag oversell — superlatives the evidence does not earn, round numbers inflated for effect, "transformational" where "useful" is true. One unearned claim discredits the earned ones around it. Round numbers conservatively: call 6.7 "over six and a half", not "nearly seven".

## The Steelman Test

Before judging the argument sound, build the strongest good-faith case against its central claim. State that counter in one or two sentences, at the height a serious opponent would put it. Then ask whether the piece survives it.

**Review for:**
- Does the author anticipate the best objection, or only weaker ones?
- If the thesis cannot withstand its strongest rebuttal, that is a Correctness or Strength failure, and it belongs in the verdict.
- Where the piece pre-empts the best counter, say so — it earns more of the reader's trust than one that ignores it.

This is the generative companion to the straw-man flag below. The straw-man row catches an author who has attacked a weak opposing view. The steelman test asks you to supply the strong one and measure the argument against it. Build the rebuttal a real opponent would make, then read the piece as that opponent would.

## The Morris Technique

Robert Morris's rule: don't say anything unless you're sure it's worth hearing.

For editorial review, apply this to each paragraph: if you deleted it, would the essay lose something the reader needs? If not, it shouldn't be there.

## Argument Structure Failures to Flag

| Pattern | Problem | Fix |
|---------|---------|-----|
| Straw man | Weakens credibility by attacking positions nobody holds | Engage the strongest version of the counterargument |
| Appeal to authority | "Experts say" without naming them | Name the expert or present the evidence directly |
| False dichotomy | "Either we do X or disaster" | Acknowledge the spectrum of options |
| Circular reasoning | The conclusion restates the premise | Identify the missing logical step |
| Anecdote as proof | One story presented as evidence of a trend | Acknowledge the anecdote's limits, add data |
| Correlation = causation | "X happened, then Y, therefore X caused Y" | Flag the leap. Suggest controls or alternative explanations |
| Moving goalposts | Definition of success shifts mid-argument | Pin down the success criteria early |
| Buried assumptions | "Obviously..." / "Clearly..." / "Everyone knows..." | Surface the assumption and test it |
