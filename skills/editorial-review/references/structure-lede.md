# Structure & Lede Reference

The single most common structural failure in thought-leadership drafts is a buried lede. Newspaper discipline — the inverted pyramid — fixes this. Apply it to articles, op-eds, essays, whitepapers, and long-form notes. Conclusion first, then supporting evidence in descending order of importance.

## The Inverted Pyramid

A reader should be able to stop at any paragraph and still have the most important information. The first sentence carries the most weight. Each succeeding sentence carries less. The piece survives being cut from the bottom.

This is the opposite of academic or narrative writing, where the payoff arrives at the end. In journalism the payoff arrives in sentence one.

## The Four Tests

### Test 1: The Title

The title is the first contract with the reader. It promises something. The body has to deliver on that promise, and the strongest articles return to the title as a frame the body picks up — explicitly or implicitly.

**Quote the title. Then ask:**

- Does it earn its place, or is it a label? "What Could Go Wrong?" carries meaning (it is the engineer's question before every deploy). "Thoughts on AI" is a label.
- Does the article deliver on the premise the title sets? A title that asks a question should be answered. A title that names a thesis should defend it. A title that promises a story should tell one.
- Does the body reference the title back, even once? Strong titles are reused as a frame inside the piece — at section breaks, at the close, or as a recurring question the body answers in stages. If the title appears only at the top and is never returned to, the article has not used the frame it set up.
- Could the title be sharpened without losing meaning? Short, concrete, specific titles outperform abstract ones. If the title contains a banned word (`landscape`, `realm`, `harnessing`, etc.), flag it.

**Flag when:**

- The title is generic ("Thoughts on X", "The Future of Y") and the article does nothing to earn it.
- The title promises a question, story, or thesis the article does not deliver on.
- The body never references the title as a frame — the title is decoration rather than load-bearing.
- The title contains an AI-voice banned word or banned phrase.

**The fix:** if the title is the right concept but underused, suggest one or two places in the body where the title's frame should be picked up (the opening sentence, a section break, the close). If the title itself is weak, propose two or three sharper alternatives, each tied to a specific line in the body.

### Test 2: The Lede

The first paragraph is no more than two sentences. The first sentence states the conclusion or the most important fact. Nothing warms up. Nothing scene-sets. Nothing previews what the piece will argue — the piece argues it, starting at word one.

**Flag when:**
- The first paragraph is three or more sentences.
- The first sentence does not contain the central claim.
- The opening is a question, a quotation, an anecdote, or a definition that delays the point.
- The reader has to reach paragraph two to learn what the piece is about.
- The lede is a "setup" sentence ("In recent years, much has been written about X") with the actual point in sentence two.

**The fix:** delete everything before the first sentence that makes a claim. If sentence three is the real lede, sentences one and two are throat-clearing — cut them.

### Test 3: The Five Ws and How

By the end of the second paragraph the reader should know:

- **Who** — which actor, company, person, team, or institution
- **What** — the action, claim, finding, or event
- **When** — the time frame or moment
- **Where** — the geography, market, or context
- **Why** — the cause or motivation
- **How** — the mechanism, method, or means

Not every dimension applies to every piece. An essay on a mental model may have no "when." A market note may have no named "who." Skip dimensions that genuinely do not apply, but flag dimensions that should apply and are missing.

**Flag when:**
- A core dimension is missing and the reader has to infer it.
- A dimension is present but vague ("recently", "some companies", "in certain markets") where a specific would land harder.
- The piece names a trend without naming the actors driving it.

### Test 4: The Cut-From-The-Bottom Test

Mentally delete the final paragraph. Does the piece still stand? Now delete the new final paragraph. Still standing? Keep going.

The point at which a deletion destroys the argument is the true end of the piece. Everything below that line is filler — usually a restatement, a sign-off, or a soft call to action.

**Flag when:**
- The final paragraph adds nothing the rest of the piece did not already establish.
- The conclusion restates the lede in different words.
- The piece ends on a generic exhortation ("organisations should consider…", "the future of X will depend on…").
- Two or more paragraphs at the bottom could be cut without loss.

## Headlines, not labels

Kenneth Roman's rule for interior headings, section breaks, and chart captions: a heading should tell the reader how to think, not name a bucket. "Low price competition is gaining" guides the reader; "Trends" makes them do the work. The same holds for chart titles — the title should state what the data shows ("Margins have halved since 2021"), not name the axis ("Margins over time").

**Flag when:**

- Section headings are category labels ("Background", "Analysis", "Considerations") rather than claims.
- A chart is titled with its subject ("Revenue by region") where it could carry its finding ("The US now drives two-thirds of revenue").
- The reader has to read the section to learn what the heading could already have told them.

**The fix:** rewrite each label as the point it introduces. A heading you cannot turn into a claim is a sign the section may not have one.

## Inverted Pyramid Anti-Patterns

| Pattern | What It Looks Like | The Problem |
|---|---|---|
| Throat-clearing lede | "In today's rapidly evolving market…" | Three sentences of warmup before the real claim. |
| Scene-setting lede | "Imagine a CFO sitting at her desk…" | Defers the point for atmosphere. Cut it. |
| Question lede | "What does the future of AI hold for finance?" | The reader came for an answer, not a question. |
| Definition lede | "Carve-outs are transactions in which…" | Reference-book opening. State the claim, define in passing. |
| Background-first | Three paragraphs of history before any insight | The newspaper convention is the reverse: insight, then history. |
| Conclusion-at-the-end | The payoff arrives in the final paragraph | Move it to sentence one. Rewrite the rest as support. |
| Restated-ending | Last paragraph paraphrases the lede | Cut the last paragraph. The lede already said it. |
| Vague sign-off | "Time will tell." / "Watch this space." | Empty filler. Cut. |

## Where the Inverted Pyramid Bends

Some long-form pieces earn a different structure — narrative essays, profiles, investigations that build to a reveal. The discipline still applies inside each section: the section opener carries the section's weight, and the reader can stop mid-section without losing the thread.

If the author has chosen a non-pyramid structure, ask whether it is earned. A 300-word op-ed has no business with a slow-burn narrative opening. A 5,000-word investigation may. When in doubt, default to the pyramid.

## How to Flag in the Review

Quote the lede. Identify which test failed. Propose a rewritten first sentence and a trimmed first paragraph. For the cut test, name the paragraph that should be the new ending.
